# Summer Cyber Fellows Operating System Class Summary

**1. Introduction:**

* **Subject:** Operating Systems
* **Instructor:** Aspen Olmsted
* **Date:** June 1st (Transcript indicates a future meeting on June 8th for further detail)

**Learning Objectives:** The primary learning objective is to understand operating systems as layers of abstraction between hardware and software, specifically focusing on how this concept applies to various domains.  Students will also develop practical C programming skills and apply the scientific method to conduct research and propose solutions within their chosen domains. The instructor employs a hands-on, project-based approach, supplemented by lectures, quizzes, and discussions, emphasizing practical application and peer learning.

**Topics Covered:** The class covers a broad range of topics, including:

* A detailed explanation of operating systems and their role as layers of abstraction.
* Practical C programming, focusing on the development of operating system-related functions.
* CPU scheduling algorithms (Priority-based preemptive scheduling, Shortest Remaining Time Next preemptive scheduling, Round Robin scheduling).
* Memory management and virtual memory management.
* Disk scheduling.
* Research methodology, including literature review, hypothesis formulation, evidence gathering, and paper writing.
* The application of operating system concepts to real-world domains and the development of domain-specific solutions.


**2. Key Topics:**

* **Textbook and Resources:** The course utilizes the Tanenbaum operating systems textbook (any edition).  Supplementary materials include recorded lectures, online C compilers (e.g., onlinegdb.com), and O'Reilly Online Learning platform (formerly Safari Online).  The instructor emphasizes the availability of multiple resources to cater to diverse learning styles.

* **Quizzes:** Quizzes are open-book, open-resource, and allow unlimited attempts.  Each attempt presents a randomized set of five multiple-choice questions requiring problem-solving skills. The instructor offers assistance with one question of each type through a Brightspace discussion board.  The quizzes are designed for learning rather than strict assessment.

* **Discussion Prompts:** Four discussion prompts are scheduled throughout the semester (Weeks 2, 4, 6, and 8).  Full credit requires responding to the prompt and two peer's responses. The instructor grades these discussions after a delay to allow sufficient participation.

* **Programming Labs:** Two course assistants (CAs) provide support for the programming labs, which are conducted in C.  Students submit their C code to Gradescope, an automated grading system that compiles and tests the code using the GNU C compiler (GCC).  Alternative C compilers (meeting the ANSI C standard) are acceptable. The instructor will provide video tutorials demonstrating the use of onlinegdb.com for those without local C compiler setups.  The labs are designed to reinforce concepts covered in lectures and the textbook, focusing on practical implementation of algorithms and data structures.  The labs include:
    * **Week 1:** Two simple C programs: "Hello World" and a leap year checker.
    * **Weeks 2-5:** Four more extensive labs covering CPU scheduling, memory allocation, virtual memory management, and disk scheduling.  The instructor will provide video walkthroughs for the first function in each lab.  A common header file (`oslabs.h`) defines data structures and function signatures used across all labs.  The instructor emphasizes testing code offline before submitting to Gradescope.  The labs involve creating and manipulating data structures in C (`struct`). Examples were provided of function implementations, such as `handle_process_arrival` for preemptive scheduling, and supporting functions to set up process data structures.


* **Research Paper:** The research paper requires students to identify a problem within their domain of expertise and propose a solution using the concepts learned in the course.  The paper should follow a standard computer science research paper format (similar to IEEE format) and consist of an extended abstract (approximately 1800 words). The instructor encourages students to leverage their unique domain expertise. The instructor stresses that the most challenging aspect of research is limiting the scope of the project. The paper will include the following sections:
    * **Introduction:** Defining the problem and its significance.
    * **Related Research:** Reviewing existing solutions and related work (minimum 3 peer-reviewed papers).
    * **Motivating Example:** A concrete scenario illustrating the problem.
    * **Hypothesis and Empirical Evidence:**  The proposed solution and supporting evidence (simulation, modeling, scanning, or open data sources).
    * **Conclusions and Future Work:** Summary of findings and potential extensions.
    * **References:**  Using IEEE format.  The instructor explains that while at least 3 peer-reviewed papers are necessary, additional non-peer-reviewed sources can be used to support the introduction.


* **Peer Review:** The class uses a peer review system called Peerceptiv.  The peer review process is integrated throughout the research paper development, with deadlines synchronized to ensure timely feedback.  Students are required to submit drafts of their paper at specific stages (Weeks 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 11) and provide peer reviews for their classmates.  Full participation in the peer review process is a significant component of the final grade.


* **Grading:**  All work is due by August 10th, with some minor scaffolding provided afterwards. The grading criteria are detailed in the syllabus and rubric, but the instructor emphasizes that consistent participation and following instructions will result in a high grade.  The instructor uses the last attempt for quiz scores.

* **Discussion Board:**  The Brightspace discussion board is used for clarifying quiz questions, seeking assistance from CAs, and engaging in general class discussions.  The instructor emphasizes the importance of participating in the discussion prompts and peer review process.

* **Common Mistakes:** The instructor anticipates that students may misinterpret problem statements or face challenges in working with C data structures.  The CAs and discussion board serve as resources to address these challenges.
